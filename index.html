A Personal Note

Peace — and the values around it

Some values sit underneath a career rather than inside it. Peace is the one that sits underneath mine — and it does not stand alone. It grows out of how we think about the future, about progress, about what we owe the people and places that come after us, and about where we refuse to let anger take us.

I come from a country that knows the cost of conflict, so peace is not an abstract word to me. It is the ground everything else stands on — families, work, trust, and the simple dignity of an ordinary day.

In my own work, peace shows up in small, practical ways: a calm voice on a difficult call, a problem solved without anger, a client who hangs up feeling heard instead of defeated. Banking runs on trust, and trust is built the way peace is — patiently, honestly, and one person at a time.

I also believe peace is a discipline, not just an absence. It has to be defended daily — in how we speak about each other, in refusing the voices that profit from division, in choosing the slower work of understanding over the quick satisfaction of blame. A society that cannot talk to itself cannot bargain with its own future.

That is the future I work toward — for my clients, my community, and my country: stability you can build a life on.

Sustainability

Nothing built on waste lasts. I believe in leaving systems — financial or otherwise — healthier than I found them: resources used with respect, institutions that endure, and progress measured by what still stands in fifty years, not by what dazzled for one quarter.

The Future

The future is not something that happens to us; it is something we are already writing, in small decisions, every day. I prefer to write it deliberately — with long horizons, honest accounting, and a hope that is disciplined rather than naive.

Progress & Development

Real progress carries people with it. Development that enriches a few while leaving communities behind is not development at all — it is extraction wearing a better suit. Growth worth having widens the circle of who gets to stand on stable ground.

Rejecting Extremism

Extremism, in any costume, is a refusal to think — it trades the hard work of coexistence for the cheap certainty of hatred. I reject it completely. Strong societies are built by people who can disagree without dehumanising, and I intend to be one of them.

Working Principles

Four principles, no exceptions

These are not slogans — they are the rules that decided real cases, real cash drawers, and real escalations across six years.

Compliance Before Convenience

KYC, AML, and FATCA/CRS checks are never the step you skip to save time. In banking, the shortcut is always the most expensive route — for the client, the bank, and your own record. Every interaction I handle passes verification first, resolution second.

Accuracy Is a Form of Respect

A balanced cash drawer, a correctly logged case, a reconciliation with zero unexplained difference — these are how a bank shows clients their money is taken seriously. I count twice, document once, and never leave a discrepancy "for tomorrow."

Explain It Like a Human

Clients rarely need more banking jargon; they need to understand what happened to their account, card, or transfer — in Arabic or English, in plain words. Clear explanation is what turns a frustrated caller into a loyal customer.

The Team Carries the Standard

As a supervisor I learned that quality is a team habit, not an individual talent. Shared checklists, honest QA feedback, and coaching after hard calls are what keep hundreds of daily interactions at one standard.

Two Systems, One Standard

Conventional and Islamic banking

Working at the Bank of Jordan (conventional) and Dubai Islamic Bank and the Syrian International Islamic Bank (Islamic) gave me both operating models from the inside: interest-based products on one side, Sharia-compliant structures on the other — with the same expectations of accuracy, documentation, and client care in both.

That dual fluency matters for employers who serve mixed client bases, and for clients who move between systems and need someone who can explain the difference honestly.

Why a Banker Writes About Philosophy

Every profession hides a philosophy

Banking's hidden philosophy is trust: the belief that strangers can cooperate across time — that a promise made today will be honoured years from now, by people who may never meet. I have spent my career inside that belief, and it has taught me to approach the larger questions the way I approach a ledger: honestly, patiently, and with respect for everything the numbers cannot show.

What follows is not theory for its own sake. These are the convictions that decide how I work, how I lead, and how I intend to live — written down because a person should be able to say what he stands for, plainly and in his own name.

Sustainability

What we owe the future

I think of sustainability the way a banker thinks of capital: it is what must still be there tomorrow for anything to be possible at all. A forest, a currency, a reputation, a community's trust — all of them can be spent quickly and rebuilt only slowly, if they can be rebuilt at all. The first duty of anyone who manages anything is to hand it over intact.

In my own field this is not abstract. Finance that extracts without renewing eventually consumes its own foundations — clients who no longer trust, markets that no longer function, institutions that exist only on paper. Sustainable banking is not a slogan about being green; it is the discipline of building things that last: sound credit, honest products, and institutions designed to outlive the people who run them.

And beyond finance, the principle is the same at every scale. We are borrowers, all of us — of land, of water, of the patience of the next generation. I want my work, and my life, to be the kind that repays more than it borrowed.

Cycles, not straight lines — what is renewed returns; what is only spent does not.

The Future

Written daily, in pencil and in ink

I do not believe the future is a place we arrive at; I believe it is a draft we are editing right now. Some of it is written in ink — the large decisions, the treaties, the technologies that change everything at once. But most of it is written in pencil, in small daily choices: how we raise children, how we treat a stranger, whether we keep a promise when keeping it costs us something.

That is why I resist both despair and complacency. Despair says the draft is finished and ruined; complacency says it writes itself. Neither is true. The honest position is responsibility: the future will be roughly as good as the care we put into the present. A teller balancing a drawer and a minister signing a treaty are doing the same kind of work at different scales — deciding, today, what tomorrow can stand on.

For my own part, I choose to act as if the future of my country and my region is bright — not because the evidence always agrees, but because acting on that belief is how evidence gets made. Hope, practised daily, is a strategy, not a mood.

Progress & Development

Growth that carries everyone

Progress is the most misused word of our age. A skyline is not progress if the people beneath it cannot afford to live in it. A growing economy is not development if it grows by leaving its own people behind. I measure progress differently: by whether ordinary life gets more dignified — whether a family can plan, a young person can learn, a small business can borrow fairly and grow.

Development, done honestly, is unglamorous. It is schools that stay open, courts that stay fair, banks that lend on merit, and officials who answer letters. I have worked in administration and in banking, and I can testify: nations are built in exactly these unremarkable rooms, by people doing unremarkable things carefully, for years.

So I am for progress — the real kind. Technology that serves people rather than replacing their dignity. Growth that widens the circle instead of raising the wall. And patience: the understanding that a society, like a good institution, is built at the speed of trust.

Step by step — real development climbs; it does not leap over people.

Peace

The ground everything stands on

I come from Syria, and I will not pretend peace is a decorative word for me. I have seen what its absence costs: not only lives, which is cost enough, but the thousand small futures that quietly disappear — the shop never opened, the degree never finished, the wedding postponed into never. War does not only destroy what exists; it steals what would have existed.

Peace, as I understand it, is not merely the silence of weapons. It is a positive condition: neighbours who can disagree without fear, institutions stronger than grudges, and a shared agreement that tomorrow is worth protecting together. Like trust in banking, it is built slowly — person by person, promise by promise — and it can be spent in a single reckless season.

That is why I practise peace at the scale I control: a calm voice in a hard conversation, a fair hearing for the other side, a refusal to pass hatred along. No one person makes peace for a nation. But nations are made of persons, and I intend to be one of the ones it can be built from.

Still water — peace is not emptiness; it is strength, settled.

Rejecting Extremism

A refusal, stated plainly

Let me be direct: I reject extremism — every kind, from every direction, wearing any flag or quoting any scripture. Extremism is not strong belief; it is the abandonment of belief's hardest part, which is living alongside people who believe differently. It offers certainty without thought, belonging without love, and victory without a future worth winning.

I have thought about why it seduces. It promises the angry a target, the hopeless a purpose, the humiliated a story in which they are heroes. You cannot defeat that with arguments alone — you defeat it by making ordinary life offer better: real dignity, real opportunity, real belonging. Every honest job, every fair institution, every teacher who takes a student seriously is counter-extremism in practice.

And for myself, the line is simple, and I hold it without apology: I will not hate a category of people. I will judge individuals by their actions, argue with ideas fiercely and people gently, and refuse — in my work, my words, and my example — to be recruited by anyone's hatred. Moderation is not weakness. It is the strength to stay human when staying human is hardest.

The line I hold — the flame is crossed out; the people on both sides remain.

My creed, in one breath: build what lasts, grow without leaving people behind, keep the peace you can reach, and refuse every hatred that asks for your signature. The rest is work — and I am happy to do it.

Your Money Is Quietly Losing Value

12 November 2026 · 6 min read

Most people think saving money means keeping it in the bank and watching the number stay the same. But that number is lying to you.

All figures in this article are in US dollars.

The silent tax

If inflation is 5% a year and your savings earn 1%, your money loses 4% of its buying power every year. After 10 years, $10,000 in savings buys what about $6,700 buys today. You didn't spend a thing — and you still got poorer.

This is not a one-off loss. It compounds, year after year, silently. Here is what happens to $10,000 left sitting in a savings account at a 4% real loss per year:

After$10,000 is worth
5 yearsabout $8,150
10 yearsabout $6,650
20 yearsabout $4,420
30 yearsabout $2,940

After 30 years, more than two-thirds of your money's value is gone — without you spending a single coin. That is the silent tax.

Growth vs. standing still — money that grows keeps pace with rising prices; money that stands still falls behind them.

Why your salary feels smaller every year

The same force shrinks your income. If your salary rises 2% in a year but prices rise 5%, you did not get a raise — you took a 3% pay cut in real terms. Your bank balance went up, but your life got more expensive faster. This is why so many people feel they are working harder yet falling behind: the numbers on the payslip grow, but what those numbers buy shrinks.

The lesson is simple: a number that does not grow faster than prices is a number going backwards — whether it is your salary or your savings.

Saving and growing are two different jobs

Saving protects you from emergencies. Growing protects you from inflation. You need both: an emergency fund for safety, and investments — even small, regular ones — for growth.

Your emergency fund comes first: 3–6 months of expenses, kept in an accessible savings account. Only after that safety net is in place should extra money go to work.

Growing your money does not mean gambling on risky bets. It means putting your money where it earns more than inflation over the long run — for most people, that means a low-cost index fund that tracks the whole market, held for years, not days. You do not need to pick winners. You need patience.

The power of starting early

Time is the ingredient most people underestimate. Look at what happens when you invest just $50 a month in a low-cost index fund earning an average of 7% a year:

AfterYou put inIt grows to
10 years$6,000about $8,650
20 years$12,000about $26,000
30 years$18,000about $61,000

After 30 years, your money has more than tripled — and two-thirds of that growth came from time and compounding, not from what you put in. The best time to start was ten years ago. The second-best time is today.

What to do this week

You do not need a perfect plan. You need four small steps:

  1. 1. Check your savings rate. Find out what your bank actually pays you — then compare it to inflation. If the gap is wide, your money is shrinking.
  2. 2. Build your emergency fund. Start with one month of expenses, then grow it to three, then six.
  3. 3. Automate a small monthly investment. Even $50 a month, invested automatically the day your salary arrives, so you never have to think about it.
  4. 4. Review once a year. Check your numbers, adjust the amount if you can, and leave the rest alone. Consistency beats cleverness.

Start small, start now

Even $50 a month in a low-cost index fund, done consistently for years, beats a savings account standing still. The amount matters less than the habit — and the earlier you start, the more time works for you instead of against you.

The question is not whether you can afford to invest. It is whether you can afford not to. What does your money do while you sleep — grow, or shrink?

Key takeaways

  • Inflation at 5% with 1% savings interest means a 4% real loss every year.
  • $10,000 left idle is worth about $6,650 in 10 years — and about $2,940 in 30.
  • A 2% raise against 5% inflation is a 3% pay cut in real terms.
  • Keep an emergency fund of 3–6 months of expenses first.
  • $50 a month at 7% grows to about $26,000 in 20 years and $61,000 in 30.
  • Invest small amounts regularly — consistency beats size.
